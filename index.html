Omega Flight Computer
Omega • IREC 2026
GALLERY CAD LAYOUT
{{ galCounter }}
Features

The official flight computer I developed for the Liberty University Rocketry Team, designed entirely in Altium. Six-layer PCB built around the STM32F4 microcontroller. Integrates a BNO055 9-DOF IMU, BMP384 barometric pressure sensor, GPS, LoRa radio, SD card, external flash chip, and battery management system. Supports multiple communication protocols to process and transmit real time telemetry data for visualization on a custom ground station GUI.

FAULTS & FIXES

• The BNO085 was the original IMU in the design. Due to challenges with the chip's SHTP during testing, I replaced it with the BNO055 to meet the closing deadline. This solved the issue, but I would like to further test the original chip and include it on another design.
• The SMT camera activation USB connector sheared on landing impact during competition. Future designs will have through-hole requirements for connectors that experience significant mechanical loads.
• The BQ29700 battery protection IC did not internally connect the Li-Ion cell ground to the main board ground. This was solved by soldering an external wire connecting the cell ground to the nearest board ground pin. More care will be taken in reviewing the BMS schematic in the future.

LESSONS LEARNED

This is my first discrete PCB to work reliably. It will serve as an invaluable resource for designing other electronics systems. I now have a much clearer understanding of impedance-controlled stackups, RF layout and routing, via shielding, power delivery, and IC supporting circuitry.

Features

Four-layer STM32-based PCB equipped with pyro channels, PWM servo control, and external headers.

FAULTS & FIXES

• VBUS sensing on the MCU was overlooked, so flashing the board over USB DFU did not work. This was a hardware issue and unsolvable on the current board. I studied USB with STM32 application notes to further understand the issue and prevent this oversight in future designs. Thankfully, I included SWD on the design in case of a critical programming failure, and that allowed me to flash the firmware. 
• Main logic level was 3.3, but the MOSFET's gate voltage threshold was 5V, so they did not fully switch. To solve this, I purchased suitable replacement components with the correct package.
• Did not place return vias near signal transitions between layers. This was also unsolvable with the current board, but I learned how currents flow and waves propagate. 

LESSONS LEARNED

Pour over datasheets for unfamiliar components. Find open-source designs to reference for the main schematic. Understand how the fields interact with nearby components and signals.

Features

Second iteration of my flight computer PCB. Uses a Teensy 4.1 with a built-in micro SD slot, BNO055 IMU, DPS310 barometric pressure sensor, pyro-channel MOSFET's, and a motor driver chip for roll control. Controls servos for TVC actuation.

FAULTS & FIXES

• Previous flight programs did not run efficiently. Sensors were grouped together and flight events were not governed by a robust state-machine. I implemented software changes to compress code, reduce blocking delays in the TVC control loop, and improve readability. 
• Removed unnecessary redundant pyro-channels to reduce complexity and free up space.

LESSONS LEARNED

I can improve hardware, but without equal refinements in software, the system will never perform to expectations.

Features

First attempt for a flight computer PCB. An Arduino Nano reads inertial measurements and barometric data, logs data to an SD card, and controls pyro channels. It also manages servos for TVC actuation.

FAULTS & FIXES

• 5V power plane supplied power for all the components, but due to signal routing, power was cut-off from a few servos. To bypass this, I soldered an external wire connecting the servos to a separate power pin. This was strictly for ground testing purposes. 
• The pyro-channel MOSFET's triggered randomly. This was identified and solved on the next prototype by adding pulldown resistors to the gates. 

LESSONS LEARNED

Always ensure proper power and grounding. Use appropriate trace widths for power and signal lines. Do not route critical signal traces parallel to each other for long distances. Space is important.

Features

First attempt for a thrust vector control rocket flight computer. An Arduino Nano Every reads inertial measurements and barometric data, logs to an SD card in flight, triggers pyro channels, and controls TVC servos. Modules are wired externally to modified breadboard rails and mounted on a compact avionics bay.

FAULTS & FIXES

• External wires were hand-soldered to each module, limiting accessibility and making them challenging to replace. I proved my design with this prototype and then transferred it to a PCB to eliminate the external wiring. 
• Space was a big constraint: 5 breakout board modules, 1 Arduino, 4 pyro-channels, status indicators, a LiPo battery, and modified breadboard rails had to fit within a 2.375" diameter airframe. The resulting bay optimized space well.

LESSONS LEARNED

Increased my understanding of basic electronics systems. Proved the schematic design for PCB development.

LAUNCHPAD & PAD AVIONICS
First electronics project · proof-of-concept
GALLERY
{{ g1.counter }}
Features

Ignition sequence control, retractable strongback with stabilization arms, launch clamps, flame trenches, and a control box with manual abort override. Supports a 3-core rocket.

FAULTS & FIXES

• Lined the flame trenches with 1/8" aluminum flat bar to protect the electrical components from the motor exhaust and curved the end of base plate to reduce the impact of direct thrust during ignition. Aluminum was not sufficient as the motors burned through within a few launches. To solve this, I replaced the aluminum stock with steel plates and added High Heat paint to the unprotected areas. This resolved the issue, allowing me to complete over 10 more launches with no visible wear to the pad. 
• Adjustable launch clamps were designed to easily accommodate vehicles of different sizes and streamline in-field pad modifications. After a few launches, the exposed servos began to corrode and act unreliably. Also, the print geometry of the slide system centralized the launch loads on two weak posts, pulling printed layers apart instead of bending across them. To solve these issues, I redesigned the launch clamps to be fixed in place, maximize the printing strength for critical components, and have a spring-loaded, retractable shield to protect the servos.
• Accessibility to critical components was a big issue, so the next launchpad design expanded the electronics space, eliminated cross-wiring between the two removable halves, and simplified the flame deflector region, moving it to the top of the pad to eliminate the need for more thermal protection for the electronics.

LESSONS LEARNED

Engineering a maximally adjustable system adds unnecessary parts, increases complexity, and provides more points of failure. Simplify as much as possible while retaining functionality, and design to directly support the current vehicle. It is much easier to redesign one aft hold-down ring for the rocket than the whole launchpad system.

TVC MOUNT
Features

Gimbaled ring motor mount driven by two MG90 servos, capable of +/- 10° X and Y corrections.

Faults & Fixes

• While there were no major issues with this mount, space and weight could have been optimized with BMS-101AMG servos and a lower 3D printing flow ratio.

Lessons Learned

The angle-based control system is insufficient for achieving perfect vehicle stability. Torque-based controllers are required for stability and future landing attempts.

CATCH POINT MECHANISM
Features

Deployable fin mechanism to enable launch tower catch attempts.

Faults & Fixes

• While there were no major issues with this design, weight could be reduced on future iterations by replacing the servo and lowering 3D printing flow ratio. The system functioned properly during flights.

Lessons Learned

This design successfully directed the force of landing into the airframe instead of the plastic structure. The fins are retracted for improved aerodynamic performance during ascent and deployed for landing.

Overview

The second prototype, built on the improvements of the first iteration, integrates a piston recovery system, upgraded avionics bay and TVC mount, and retractable catch points. It has completed over three successful flights aimed at tuning the control system. Inspired by SpaceX's Starship, the final goal of this rocket is to autonomously navigate back to the pad for a tower catch. The project is currently on hold as I pursue other personal projects.

TVC Mount
Features

Gimbaled motor mount driven by two vertically stacked SG90 servos, capable of +/- 15° X and Y corrections.

Faults & Fixes

• Heat from the motor softened the plastic housing, reducing responsiveness and leading to unnecessary repairs. To solve this, I insulated the housing with a 2 mm cork sleeve, and it worked perfectly.
• The spherical bearing allowed the motor housing to rotate around the Z axis. During launches, motor thrust caused gimbal misalignments, so the servos' actuation was not directly along the X and Y axes. This proved to be an issue for properly tuning the PD controller during ground testing. As a temporary solution, I strengthened the push rods to inhibit the extra rotation. This worked fine for a few flights, and allowed me to still gather useful data. I developed a more robust, ring-gimbal solution for the next prototype.

Lessons Learned

Because I was constrained by the 2.375" diameter airframe, I decided to stack the servos vertically, using a spherical bearing for the gimbal. This eliminated the need for airframe cutouts, significantly improving structural stability. It was a great idea in theory, mirroring modern TVC gimbals, but the design had too many points of failure and did not prove to be mass-efficient. I will work to refine this design in the future. 

Landing Legs
Features

Entirely 3D printed landing leg mechanism aimed at reducing the cost, weight, and elasticity of other designs employing carbon fiber struts. Developed for drop testing purposes only and as a proof-of-concept for future prototypes.

Faults & Fixes

• On a few occasions, the strut mounting ring failed from the force of landing and damaged the airframe. The solution was strengthening the ring and moving it up to reduce the direct radial force into the airframe.

Lessons Learned

My attempts at developing a lighter alternative to a carbon fiber landing leg mechanism were unsuccessful. My most reliable design was heavier and slightly weaker. Ultimately, I decided not to fly with this mechanism and pursue something new on the next prototype.

Overview

My first thrust vector control rocket, built as a cost-effective and reusable test platform, integrated a piston recovery system, avionics bay, TVC motor mount, and deployable landing legs. Every component was custom manufactured, including the airframe, which I spiral-wound by hand out of kraft paper. It completed over five successful flights, revealing critical issues that ultimately led to its loss but provided important data and design revisions that shaped the next prototype.

Mousetrap Car

2025
Overview

A mousetrap car I built for the distance and speed events in an introductory engineering course at Liberty University. I designed an A-frame structure that loops the string over a roller when the arm is vertical, keeping the angle of the string and the arm between 45° and 90° through the entire rotation. The moment arm never falls below 71% of its maximum, so the string keeps pulling until the end of the rotation. In standard models, that angle reaches 0° at the end of the rotation, so the arm stops pulling string and the spring's remaining energy is wasted. The resulting string length added 0.25 m of powered distance in the speed event and 1.70 m in the distance event. The car won the final competition across all class sections and the Design Award judged by graduate students. Future versions should reduce overall weight of the car and optimize the string's path, keeping the angle between the string and the arm at 90° throughout the entire rotation to minimize wasted energy from the spring.

About

I am a committed Mechanical Engineering student at Liberty University with a minor in Physics driven by a deep personal passion for rocketry and solving the most complex challenges. With proficiency in a variety of CAD, PCB design, and programming tools, I am fully self-taught, enabling me to pursue ambitious goals.

Some of my personal projects include developing and flying a thrust-vector control model rocket, complete with a launchpad and custom avionics. Currently, I am working on a regeneratively-cooled bipropellant liquid rocket engine and hope to complete it by Summer 2027.

With this as my foundation, I am excited to contribute to the Liberty Rocketry Team as the Chief Engineer for our 2027 vehicle, Exodus. I look forward to using my skills to further the program and gain invaluable experience in the industry.
